**Key facts:**
- Optional Settings are entered by pressing the Main Control Knob; turn it to scroll, press to select, turn to adjust.
- Hot Start – adjusts amperage at start of weld.
- Arc Force – adjusts weld penetration and smoothness.
- Recall Setting – retrieves saved settings.
- Save Setting – program up to 5 different configurations.
- Protective gear minimum: shade number 10 full face shield (or welding mask), ear protection, welding gloves, sleeves and apron, NIOSH-approved respirator, fire-resistant work clothes without pockets. Do not breathe arc fumes.
- Step 8: place the bare metal end of the Stick Electrode (sold separately) inside the jaws of the Electrode Holder.
- Arc ignition tips: (a) tap the surface with the Electrode, (b) stroke the surface with the Electrode, (c) strike the surface like a match with the Electrode.
- Step 10 (after arc ignites): lift the Electrode off the workpiece the same distance as the diameter of the bare metal end; tilt Electrode back 10 to 20 degrees; drag Electrode to the back end of the weld puddle to deposit material as needed.
- Step 11: when finished, lift the Electrode from the workpiece, then set the Electrode Holder down on a nonconductive, nonflammable surface away from grounded objects.
- Step 12: turn the Power Switch OFF. Step 13 (after use): allow Welder to cool down, unplug power cord, remove Ground Clamp, disconnect Electrode Holder and Ground Cables. Step 14: clean and store Welder and accessories indoors out of children's reach.
- Note: initial settings may need adjustment after stopping and inspecting the weld; proper welding takes experience.

## Owner Manual Page 34
**Content:** "Welding Tips" intro: how to evaluate welds after cooling and slag removal, the Strike Test procedure for destructively testing a practice weld on scrap, and how to clean a weld (chipping hammer, wire brush, angle grinder).

**Tables:** None on this page.

**Diagrams & images:**
- Boxed callout (top center): "After practice welding a couple of welding beads, STOP and examine your weld using the following guidelines."
- Strike Test illustrations (right side, two stacked diagrams):
  - Top diagram: a dead-blow hammer striking the free end of a clamped "SCRAP workpiece" held in a vise by a "clamp"; caption "GOOD WELD bends and is not brittle" — the weld bead flexes without cracking.
  - Bottom diagram: same setup (labels "dead-blow hammer", "clamp", "SCRAP workpiece") with caption "POOR WELD snaps or cracks" — the weld fractures at the joint.
- Cleaning the Weld illustration (right side): photo of a combination tool labeled "Chipping Hammer" (hammer head at top) and "Wire Brush" (bristles along the handle).
- WARNING icon panel (safety-goggles graphic) with text: "TO PREVENT SERIOUS INJURY: Continue to wear ANSI-approved safety goggles and protective wear when cleaning a weld. Sparks or chips may fly when cleaning."

**Key facts:**
- Wear ANSI-approved safety goggles during the Strike Test.
- CAUTION: the Strike Test WILL damage the weld it is performed on; it is ONLY an indicator of weld technique and is not intended to test working welds.
- Strike Test procedure: (1) after two scraps have been welded together and the weld has cooled, clamp one scrap in a sturdy vise; (2) stay clear from underneath while striking the opposite scrap with a heavy hammer, preferably a dead-blow hammer; (3) a GOOD WELD will deform but not break; a POOR WELD will be brittle and snap at the weld.
- NOTICE: TIG welding is a complicated process requiring experience and skill; training beyond the scope of this manual is required to TIG weld properly.
- Cleaning: flux-cored wire and stick welds are covered by slag — knock it off with a chipping hammer, being careful not to damage the weld or base material; then use a wire brush to further clean, or an angle grinder (sold separately) to shape the weld.

## Owner Manual Page 36
**Content:** "Wire Weld Penetration (Workpiece Heat Control)" — detailed possible-causes-and-solutions lists for excess penetration/burn-through and inadequate penetration, plus sections on Bend at Joint, Coat of Slag Over Weld, and Weld Not Adhering Properly (wire welding).

**Tables:** None (all content is in headed cause/solution lists, transcribed under Key facts).

**Diagrams & images:**
- "PROFILE VIEWS" row of three cross-sections: EXCESS PENETRATION OR BURN-THROUGH ("Weld droops on top and underneath, or falls through entirely, making a hole."), PROPER PENETRATION ("Weld is visible underneath and bulges slightly on top."), INADEQUATE PENETRATION ("Weld does not penetrate the joint fully, just on the surface."). Arrows point from the excess and inadequate profiles down to their respective POSSIBLE CAUSES AND SOLUTIONS lists.
- "Bend at Joint" — PROFILE VIEW cross-section showing two workpiece halves angled/bent at the welded joint.
- "Coat of Slag Over Weld" — TOP VIEW photo of a weld covered by dark slag with part chipped away; caption "PARTIALLY CHIPPED AWAY TO SHOW WELD".
- "Weld Not Adhering Properly" — PROFILE VIEW cross-section showing gaps (circled/dotted highlight areas) between the weld and the previous bead and between the weld and the workpiece.

**Key facts:**
- Excess penetration / burn-through causes: (1) Workpiece overheating — reduce wire feed speed, decrease weld current; (2) Travel speed too slow — increase travel speed and keep it steady; (3) Excessive material at weld — reduce wire feed speed.
- Inadequate penetration causes: (4) Incorrect welding technique — maintain 1/2" or less CTWD, keep arc on leading edge of weld puddle, hold MIG Gun at proper angles; (5) Insufficient weld heat — reduce travel speed, increase weld current; (6) Workpieces too thick/close — bevel thick workpieces, allow slight gap, weld in several passes; (7) Insufficient weld material — increase wire feed speed.
- Bend at Joint causes: (1) Improper clamping — clamp workpieces securely, make tack welds to hold workpieces; (2) Excessive heat — weld a small portion and allow to cool before proceeding, increase travel speed, reduce wire feed speed.
- Coat of Slag Over Weld: slag is a necessary part of a flux-cored wire weld; it shields the weld from impurities — clean it off with a Chipping Hammer and Wire Brush after welding. Gas-shielded MIG welds are protected by the shielding gas and do not need slag to protect them.
- Weld Not Adhering Properly causes: (1) Incorrect welding technique — place stringer bead at correct place in joint, adjust workpiece position or weld angle to permit proper welding to bottom of piece, pause briefly at sides during weave bead, keep arc on leading edge of weld puddle, hold MIG Gun at proper angles; (2) Insufficient weld heat — increase current, increase wire feed speed; (3) Dirty workpiece — clean workpiece down to bare metal; (4) Insufficient weld material — increase wire feed speed; (5) Workpiece gap too narrow — widen groove or increase bevel.

**Key facts:**
- Porosity causes: (1) Incorrect polarity — check that polarity is set correctly for type of welding; (2) Insufficient shielding gas (MIG only) — increase flow of gas, clean nozzle, maintain proper CTWD; (3) Incorrect shielding gas (MIG only) — use shielding gas recommended by wire supplier; (4) Dirty workpiece or welding wire — clean workpiece down to bare metal, make certain wire is clean and free from oil, coatings, and other residues; (5) Inconsistent travel speed — maintain steady travel speed; (6) CTWD too long — reduce CTWD.
- Excessive spatter causes: (1) Dirty workpiece or welding wire — clean workpiece down to bare metal, ensure wire is clean and free of oil/coatings/residues; (2) Incorrect polarity — check polarity is set correctly for type of welding; (3) Insufficient shielding gas (MIG only) — increase flow of gas, clean nozzle, maintain proper CTWD; (4) Wire feeding too fast — reduce wire feed speed; (5) CTWD too long — reduce CTWD.
- Crooked/Wavy Bead causes: (1) Inaccurate welding — use two hands or rest hand on steady surface; (2) Inconsistent travel speed — maintain steady travel speed; (3) CTWD too long — reduce CTWD.
- Burn-Through causes: (1) Workpiece overheating — reduce current and/or wire feed speed; (2) Travel speed too slow — increase travel speed and keep it steady; (3) Excessive material at weld — reduce wire feed speed.
